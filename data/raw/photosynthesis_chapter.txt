# CHAPTER: PHOTOSYNTHESIS IN HIGHER PLANTS

## 1. Introduction and Overview of Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the fundamental biological process by which autotrophic organisms, such as green plants, algae, and cyanobacteria, convert radiant solar energy into chemical energy stored within organic molecules. This process is essential for maintaining life on Earth, as it produces atmospheric oxygen (O2) and provides the primary organic carbon source for heterotrophic organisms.

The overall chemical equation summarizing oxygenic photosynthesis is represented as:
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + Light Energy --> C6H12O6 + 6 O2

Photosynthesis consists of two interconnected metabolic stages occurring within specialized cellular organelles called chloroplasts:
1. The Light-Dependent Reactions (solar energy harvesting and chemical conversion to ATP and NADPH).
2. The Light-Independent Reactions, also known as the Calvin Cycle (carbon dioxide assimilation and synthesis of triose phosphates/glucose).

---

## 2. Chloroplast Structure and Organization

Chloroplasts are double-membrane-bound organelles found predominantly in the mesophyll cells of plant leaves. The structural organization of a chloroplast is optimized for energy capture, electron transport, and enzymatic catalysis:

- Outer Membrane and Inner Membrane: The outer membrane is permeable to small molecules, whereas the inner membrane is selectively permeable, regulating substrate transport into the interior matrix.
- Stroma: The dense fluid-filled matrix enclosed by the inner membrane. The stroma contains soluble enzymes responsible for the Calvin Cycle, chloroplast DNA (cpDNA), ribosomes, and starch granules.
- Thylakoid System: An internal membrane network composed of flattened disc-like sacs called thylakoid membranes. Thylakoid membranes enclose an internal compartment called the thylakoid lumen.
- Grana (singular: Granum): Stacks of thylakoids interconnected by unstacked membranes called stroma lamellae (or intergranal lamellae).
- Localization of Machinery: Photosystem II (PSII) is predominantly localized in the appressed regions of grana thylakoids, whereas Photosystem I (PSI) and ATP Synthase complexes are localized in non-appressed regions (stroma lamellae and outer grana margins) exposed to the stroma.

---

## 3. Photosynthetic Pigments and Light Absorption

Light absorption is mediated by photosynthetic pigments embedded within the thylakoid membranes. These pigments absorb specific wavelengths of visible light (400 nm to 700 nm, known as Photosynthetically Active Radiation or PAR):

- Chlorophyll a: The primary photosynthetic pigment present in all oxygenic phototrophs. Chlorophyll a has maximum absorption peaks in the blue (~430 nm) and red (~660 nm) regions of the spectrum. The reaction center chlorophyll a molecules are designated P680 for PSII and P700 for PSI.
- Chlorophyll b: An accessory pigment absorbing blue-green (~450 nm) and red-orange (~640 nm) light. It expands the spectrum of light usable for photosynthesis and transfers absorbed energy to chlorophyll a via resonance energy transfer.
- Carotenoids: Accessory pigments including carotenes (orange/red) and xanthophylls (yellow). Carotenoids absorb blue-green light (400-500 nm) and perform photoprotection by dissipating excess energy as heat (non-photochemical quenching), preventing oxidative damage to chlorophyll molecules.

## 4. Light-Dependent Reactions and Photophosphorylation

The light-dependent reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes and convert solar energy into chemical reducing power (NADPH) and cellular energy currency (ATP).

### 4.1 Photolysis of Water and Photosystem II (PSII)
1. Photon Absorption: Light energy is absorbed by the Light-Harvesting Complexes (LHC II) and transferred to the reaction center P680.
2. Charge Separation: Excited P680 loses an electron to the primary electron acceptor pheophytin, generating P680+ (a powerful oxidizing agent).
3. Water Splitting: P680+ extracts electrons from water molecules via the Oxygen-Evolving Complex (OEC), containing a manganese-calcium cluster (Mn4CaO5). The photolysis reaction is:
   2 H2O --> 4 H+ + 4 e- + O2
   Protons (H+) accumulate inside the thylakoid lumen, contributing to a transmembrane proton gradient, while O2 is released as a byproduct.

### 4.2 Electron Transport Chain (ETC)
1. Plastoguinone (PQ): Pheophytin transfers electrons to plastoquinone (PQ), which picks up protons from the stroma and shuttles electrons to the Cytochrome b6f complex.
2. Cytochrome b6f Complex: As electrons pass through Cytochrome b6f, protons are pumped from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen, intensifying the proton motive force.
3. Plastocyanin (PC): Electrons are transferred from Cytochrome b6f to plastocyanin (PC), a copper-containing soluble protein in the lumen, which delivers electrons to Photosystem I (PSI).

### 4.3 Photosystem I (PSI) and NADPH Synthesis
1. P700 Oxidation: PSI reaction center P700 absorbs photons (or receives energy from LHC I), ejecting high-energy electrons through a series of acceptors (A0, A1, iron-sulfur centers FX, FA, FB).
2. Ferredoxin (Fd): Electrons are transferred to ferredoxin, a iron-sulfur protein located on the stromal side of the thylakoid membrane.
3. NADP+ Reductase: Ferredoxin-NADP+ reductase (FNR) catalyzes the reduction of NADP+ to NADPH using electrons from Fd and protons from the stroma:
   NADP+ + 2 H+ + 2 e- --> NADPH + H+

### 4.4 ATP Synthesis via Chemiosmosis
The accumulation of H+ in the thylakoid lumen (from photolysis and PQ proton pumping) establishes a steep electrochemical proton gradient (pH difference across the thylakoid membrane). Protons flow down their electrochemical gradient back into the stroma through the CF0-CF1 ATP Synthase enzyme complex. This proton flux drives the rotational catalysis of ATP Synthase, phosphorylating ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi) into ATP:
ADP + Pi --> ATP (Photophosphorylation)

### 4.5 Non-Cyclic vs Cyclic Photophosphorylation
- Non-Cyclic Photophosphorylation (Z-Scheme): Involves both PSII and PSI operating in series. Produces both ATP and NADPH along with O2.
- Cyclic Photophosphorylation: Involves PSI only. When NADP+ supply is low or ATP requirement exceeds NADPH supply, electrons from ferredoxin flow back to plastoquinone and Cytochrome b6f instead of NADP+, pumping protons and synthesizing ATP without producing NADPH or releasing O2.

## 5. Light-Independent Reactions (The Calvin Cycle)

The light-independent reactions occur in the stroma of the chloroplast and utilize ATP and NADPH synthesized during light reactions to reduce carbon dioxide into carbohydrates. The Calvin Cycle operates in three distinct enzymatic phases:

### Phase 1: Carbon Fixation
Carbon dioxide (CO2) enters the stroma and combines with a 5-carbon acceptor molecule, Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP). This carboxylation reaction is catalyzed by the enzyme Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase-oxygenase (Rubisco), the most abundant protein on Earth.
The resulting unstable 6-carbon intermediate immediately splits into two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA), a 3-carbon compound (hence termed the C3 pathway).

### Phase 2: Reduction Phase
Each 3-PGA molecule undergoes phosphorylation by ATP (catalyzed by phosphoglycerate kinase) to yield 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate. Subsequently, NADPH reduces 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (catalyzed by glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) to form Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P / GAP), a 3-carbon sugar.
For every 3 CO2 molecules fixed, 6 G3P molecules are produced. However, only 1 net G3P molecule exits the cycle to form glucose or sucrose, while the remaining 5 G3P molecules are recycled.

### Phase 3: Regeneration of RuBP
The remaining 5 G3P molecules undergo complex enzymatic rearrangements (involving transketolase and aldolase enzymes) to regenerate 3 molecules of the 5-carbon RuBP. This regeneration phase requires an additional 3 ATP molecules.

### Energy Summary of the Calvin Cycle
To synthesize one molecule of a 6-carbon glucose (C6H12O6), 6 turns of the Calvin Cycle are required, fixing 6 CO2 molecules and consuming:
- 18 ATP molecules (12 in reduction phase, 6 in regeneration phase)
- 12 NADPH molecules

## 6. Variations in Carbon Fixation Pathways: C3, C4, and CAM

While C3 plants (e.g., wheat, rice, spinach) directly fix CO2 via Rubisco in mesophyll cells, photorespiration can reduce photosynthetic efficiency under high temperatures or low CO2 conditions because Rubisco also acts as an oxygenase (combining RuBP with O2 to form phosphoglycolate).

### 6.1 The C4 Pathway (Hatch-Slack Pathway)
C4 plants (e.g., maize, sugarcane, sorghum) suppress photorespiration through spatial separation of initial carbon fixation and the Calvin Cycle:
- Anatomy: C4 leaves possess specialized Kranz Anatomy, featuring ring-like mesophyll cells surrounding dense bundle sheath cells.
- Initial Fixation: In mesophyll cells, CO2 (as HCO3-) is fixed by Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) carboxylase into a 4-carbon organic acid, Oxaloacetate (OAA). PEP carboxylase has no oxygenase activity and high affinity for bicarbonate.
- Transport and Decarboxylation: OAA is reduced to malate and transported into bundle sheath cells. In bundle sheath cells, malate is decarboxylated to release CO2 at high concentrations directly around Rubisco, driving the Calvin Cycle efficiently while inhibiting oxygenase activity.

### 6.2 The CAM Pathway (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism)
CAM plants (e.g., pineapple, cacti, succulents) adapt to arid environments through temporal separation of carbon fixation:
- Night (Stomata Open): CAM plants open stomata at night to absorb CO2, fixing it via PEP carboxylase into malate. Malate is stored in large central vacuoles as malic acid.
- Day (Stomata Closed): During daytime, stomata remain closed to prevent transpiration water loss. Stored malic acid leaves the vacuole, is decarboxylated in the stroma to release CO2, and feeds Rubisco and the Calvin Cycle powered by ATP and NADPH from daytime light reactions.

## 7. Factors Affecting Photosynthetic Rate

The rate of photosynthesis is governed by Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors, which states that when a chemical process is conditioned by several factors, its rate is limited by the factor present at the slowest rate.

1. Light Intensity and Quality: At low light intensity, photosynthetic rate increases linearly with light intensity. At high light intensity, photosynthesis reaches a saturation point. Excess light can lead to photoinhibition and photodamage of PSII.
2. Carbon Dioxide Concentration: CO2 is usually the major limiting factor in atmospheric conditions (~0.04% or 400 ppm). Increasing CO2 concentration boosts C3 photosynthetic rates substantially up to saturation (~500 ppm).
3. Temperature: Light reactions are relatively temperature-insensitive, whereas enzymatic Calvin Cycle reactions are highly temperature-dependent. Optimum temperature ranges vary by pathway (20-25 °C for C3 plants vs 30-45 °C for C4 plants).
4. Water Availability: Water stress causes stomatal closure, reducing internal CO2 availability, causing leaf wilting, and slowing photosynthetic metabolism.
